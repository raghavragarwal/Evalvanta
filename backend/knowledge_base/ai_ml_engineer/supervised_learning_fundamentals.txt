Supervised Learning Fundamentals

Supervised learning is the task of learning a function that maps inputs to outputs from a set of labeled examples. The central assumption is that the training data is drawn from the same distribution as the data the model will eventually see in production; when that assumption breaks -- known as distribution shift -- a model's measured training or validation performance stops being a reliable predictor of real-world performance. This is why splitting data into training, validation, and test sets, and being disciplined about never letting information from the validation or test sets leak into training, is not just procedural hygiene but a direct safeguard against overestimating how well a model will generalize.

The bias-variance tradeoff describes the two fundamental ways a model can fail to generalize well. High bias means the model is too simple to capture the underlying pattern in the data -- it underfits, performing poorly on both training and test data. High variance means the model is sensitive to the specific noise in the training set -- it overfits, performing very well on training data but poorly on unseen data. Model complexity is the main lever here: increasing complexity (more parameters, deeper trees, higher-degree polynomial features) typically reduces bias but increases variance. Regularization techniques such as L1 and L2 penalties, dropout, or early stopping are ways of deliberately constraining a model's effective complexity to manage this tradeoff, even when the model architecture itself has plenty of raw capacity to overfit.

Loss functions define what "good" means for a given task, and the choice of loss function is itself a modeling decision, not just a mathematical detail. Mean squared error penalizes large errors disproportionately (because the error is squared), which is appropriate when large deviations are especially costly, but makes the loss sensitive to outliers. Cross-entropy loss, used for classification, directly measures the divergence between the predicted probability distribution over classes and the true distribution, and its gradient behaves well even when a prediction is confidently wrong, which is part of why it's preferred over squared error for classification despite both being technically usable.

Evaluation metrics need to match the actual cost structure of the problem, not just measure raw accuracy. On an imbalanced dataset -- for example, fraud detection where fraudulent transactions are rare -- a model that always predicts "not fraud" can achieve very high accuracy while being completely useless. Precision (of the things flagged positive, how many actually were) and recall (of the things that were actually positive, how many did the model catch) capture different failure modes, and the right balance between them depends on the relative cost of false positives versus false negatives in the specific application. The F1 score balances precision and recall into a single number, but for real decision-making it's often more useful to look at the full precision-recall tradeoff curve and pick an operating threshold deliberately, rather than defaulting to the 0.5 probability cutoff.

Cross-validation, most commonly k-fold cross-validation, addresses the problem that a single train/validation split gives a noisy estimate of generalization performance, especially with limited data. By partitioning the data into k folds and rotating which fold serves as the validation set while training on the rest, cross-validation produces k performance estimates whose average is a more stable measure of expected generalization, and whose spread gives a sense of how sensitive the model's performance is to exactly which examples it happened to be trained or evaluated on.
